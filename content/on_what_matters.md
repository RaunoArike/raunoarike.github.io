You're not alone if your first reaction to this is "Parfit, the ultimate champion and advocate of moral realism, calls his view non-realist??" While a closer look at the taxonomy reassures us that Parfit did believe in the existence of irreducibly normative truths, the non-realist in Non-Realist Cognitivism is worth pausing on—it clears up an important misconception that I and, I suspect, many others have held about his position.

Before reading this book, I went around telling people that the proper form of moral realism that Parfit endorsed and that anti-realists primarily need to argue against separates the following three ontological categories:

- Natural facts—facts that ...
- Mathematical facts—facts that ...
- Normative facts—facts that ...

This, I have learned, is a bad strawman of Parfit's position. Parfit calls his position non-realist specifically to distinguish it from the Platonic view that asserts that all the aforementioned ontological categories exist. Parfit claims that it is unclear what it would mean for our ontology to contain weighty categories outside of the natural, and leaves the burden of providing the explanation on the Metaphysical Non-Naturalists who assert that such ontological categories exist. Thus, Parfit also claims that the existence of mathematical and logical facts doesn't have any thorny ontological implications. This is an important feature of Parfit's view that demands further elucidation, and I will return to it soon. First, however, we need to answer a simpler question: how does Parfit defend the existence of objective morality without asserting the existence of a normative ontological realm?



## Ontological implications of irreducibly normative facts

Parfit's critics say that one can't have a free lunch: either you accept that there are no irreducibly normative facts, or you accept that there are "curious metaphysical objects like Plato’s forms, which are entities of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the Universe".

Is Parfit justified in speaking of truths that don't have a place in our ontologies? To answer this question, I will go on an interlude to volume II of On What Matters, which is where Parfit discusses this question more extensively. Parfit begins by discussing the existence of numbers and similar abstract entities, which, though different from normative reasons, face similar objections to their existence. We can ask:

> Do numbers really exist in the fundamental, ontological sense, though they do not exist in space or time?

*Platonists*, who ..., would say yes. *Nominalists*, who ..., would say no. Parfit instead endorses the No Clear Question View: this problem is too unclear to even have an answer.

To decide whether it is, let's first discuss what it means for a true statement to not be ontologically weighty. Parfit is frustratingly vague with regards to this matter, so I'll go through multiple different possibilities.




If we assert the existence of something that doesn't belong to our ontology, what does it mean for that thing to exist? In order to answer these questions, it is helpful to go on a quick interlude to a century ago when the likes of Russell, Ramsey, and Wittgenstein were grappling with a closely related question: what is the ontological status of logical truths? Russell and Frege held Platonic positions: Russell said that logical truths are fully general facts about the universe, while Frege thought they described a realm of abstract logical objects. These positions face an obvious issue: if logic states facts, it's conceivable that these facts could have been otherwise, but it feels incorrect to regard logical truths as contingent.

In the *Tractatus*, Wittgenstein responded to this by denying that logical propositions state anything at all. A normal proposition divides the space of possibilities in two: the situations that make it true and situations that make it false. Tautologies are true under any truth assignment, and therefore a degenerate case where there are no situations that can make them false. Logic is necessary because tautologies cannot fail, and knowable a priori because ...

Ramsey agreed with Wittgenstein on logical truths, but argued that Wittgenstein failed to recognize the natural extension of this idea to mathematics. He considered a contingent mathematics as intolerable as a contingent logic, and set out to repair Russell's *Principia* at the points where it smuggled in non-tautological content, namely the axiom of reducibility and Russell's definition of identity.

It is certainly true that it is useful for humans to have access to the concepts of rightness and wrongness. As Parfit says, beliefs about these concepts help us make good decisions and act well. However, making good decisions and acting well are in themselves normative notions. The fact that such concepts help humans make good decisions need not mean that an AI, trying to provide a comprehensive account of our actions, would need to make use of those concepts. However, if those concepts are an objective feature of a situation, they would necessarily have to be part of any comprehensive description of that situation.

Railton provides a helpful framing for thinking about this (and one that Parfit, to the best of my knowledge, endorses):

    The concept <rightness> and the concept <minimizing suffering> have the same extension, and thus present the same extensional or ontological property, being suffering-minimizing, under different guises.[^1]

Notice the word *guises*. We use different guises for the same concept because those different guises are useful for simplifying or organizing our thinking.

One could counter that this different guise is a necessary extension of the ontological property, similarly to how mathematical truths can be seen as being necessary extensions of ontological properties. For example, from the ontological properties of ..., one could derive the properties of all natural numbers. This derivation isn't necessary to possess a comprehensive description of the objects that those ontological properties refer to, but ... Similarly, ...

One way to clean up the confusion is to notice that Parfit talks of a single well-defined supervenience relationship between natural properties and normative facts. There must be some ontological and natural property (or properties) that makes acts have the non-ontological and non-natural property of being right. The nihilist would say that there is no such single correct relationship.

As Gibbard says: "Is there a fact that water is H2O, distinct from the trivial fact that water is water? ... By 'facts' ... do we mean states of affairs that obtain—so that there's just one fact here? ... Or by 'facts' do we mean true thoughts, so that it's one fact that water is water and another fact that water is H2O? If so, then perhaps we have seen that there are, after all, normative facts distinct from all natural facts."
